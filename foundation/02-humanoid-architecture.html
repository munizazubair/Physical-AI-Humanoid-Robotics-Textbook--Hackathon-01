Chapter 2: Humanoid Robot Architecture Overview

Learning Objectives​

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Identify the three major subsystems of a humanoid robot (perception, planning, control)
  • Explain how these subsystems integrate to enable intelligent behavior
  • Describe the data flow from sensors to actuators in a complete robotic system
  • Recognize the role of middleware in coordinating complex robotic systems

Introduction​

A humanoid robot is not a monolithic program—it's a complex system of interconnected subsystems, each responsible for a specific aspect of intelligent behavior. Consider a humanoid robot walking through a crowded room to retrieve an object. It must:

  • See the room layout, obstacles, and target object (perception)
  • Decide how to navigate around people and furniture (planning)
  • Execute smooth, balanced walking motions (control)
  • Adapt if someone steps in its path or the object is moved

Each of these capabilities requires sophisticated algorithms and real-time coordination. How do these pieces fit together? How does visual data from cameras translate into motor commands that move joints?

In this chapter, we explore the three-layer architecture that structures most humanoid robots: the perception layer (sensing and understanding), the planning layer (decision-making and reasoning), and the control layer (executing actions). We'll examine how data flows between these layers and how middleware—software that connects subsystems—enables seamless integration.

By understanding this architecture, you'll recognize the blueprint that underlies modern Physical AI systems, from service robots to advanced humanoids.

Three-Layer Architecture​

Humanoid robots are typically organized into three functional layers, each building on the one below:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           Planning Layer                    │
│  (Task planning, motion planning, logic)    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│           Perception Layer                  │
│  (Sensing, filtering, state estimation)     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│           Control Layer                     │
│  (Motor control, balance, low-level actuation)│
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
         ▲                        │
         │   Sensor Data          │ Motor Commands
         │                        ▼
    [Sensors]                [Actuators]

Figure 2.1: The three-layer architecture of a humanoid robot. Sensors feed data upward to perception, which informs planning, which sends commands downward to control and actuators.

Let's explore each layer in detail.

Perception Layer: Sensors and Sensing​

The perception layer is responsible for sensing the environment and building an internal representation of the world. It processes raw sensor data and extracts meaningful information: "Where is the table?" "Is that object a cup?" "Am I tilted to the left?"

Key Functions:

  1. Sensor Fusion: Combining data from multiple sensors (cameras, LiDAR, IMUs, force sensors) to form a coherent picture
  2. Filtering and Noise Reduction: Removing sensor noise, outliers, and artifacts to produce reliable measurements
  3. State Estimation: Determining the robot's own state (position, orientation, joint angles, velocity) and the state of the environment (object locations, surface geometry)

Example: A humanoid with cameras, depth sensors, and an IMU uses the perception layer to:

  • Detect obstacles and free space for navigation
  • Recognize objects (cups, doors, furniture)
  • Estimate its own tilt and balance using IMU data
  • Fuse visual and depth data to localize objects in 3D space

Perception is the foundation. Without accurate sensing, planning and control operate on false information, leading to collisions, falls, or task failures.

Planning Layer: Decision Making and Reasoning​

The planning layer takes the world model from perception and decides what to do next. It answers high-level questions: "How do I reach the kitchen?" "Which grasp will work best for this object?" "Should I wait for this person to pass before moving?"

Key Functions:

  1. Task Planning: Breaking down high-level goals (e.g., "fetch the cup") into sequences of actions (navigate to table, reach for cup, grasp cup, return)
  2. Motion Planning: Computing collision-free paths for the robot's arms, body, or entire system
  3. Behavior Arbitration: Choosing between competing objectives (e.g., "reach goal quickly" vs. "avoid startling nearby humans")

Example: A humanoid robot tasked with delivering a cup of water uses the planning layer to:

  • Plan a navigation path through the room, avoiding obstacles
  • Compute an arm trajectory that reaches the cup without hitting the table
  • Decide when to slow down near humans for safety
  • Re-plan if the environment changes (a chair is moved, a person walks in front)

Planning operates at a slower timescale than perception or control—typically 1-10 Hz—because it involves search, optimization, or reasoning over complex action spaces.

Control Layer: Actuation and Execution​

The control layer translates high-level commands from planning into low-level motor signals that drive actuators. It ensures that the robot executes motions smoothly, stably, and safely.

Key Functions:

  1. Joint-Level Control: Sending torque or position commands to individual motors to achieve desired joint angles
  2. Whole-Body Control: Coordinating multiple joints to maintain balance, distribute forces, and achieve complex motions (like walking or reaching)
  3. Feedback Control: Continuously adjusting motor commands based on sensor feedback to correct errors and maintain stability

Example: When the planning layer outputs "move arm to position X," the control layer:

  • Computes inverse kinematics to determine required joint angles
  • Applies PID control (or more advanced methods like model predictive control) to drive motors toward those angles
  • Monitors joint encoders and adjusts torques in real-time to compensate for friction, gravity, and external forces
  • Ensures the motion is smooth (no jerky movements) and safe (limits applied forces)

Control operates at the fastest timescale—often 100-1000 Hz—because physical dynamics evolve quickly. A walking robot must adjust balance every few milliseconds to avoid falling.

Data Flow Between Layers​

How does information move through these layers? Let's trace the data flow in a humanoid robot performing a simple task: picking up a cup.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                      Data Flow Diagram                       │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                              │
│   Cameras ──┐                                               │
│   Depth  ───┼──> [Perception]                               │
│   IMU    ───┘      │                                        │
│                    │ Object location, robot pose            │
│                    v                                        │
│              [Planning]                                     │
│                    │ Arm trajectory, grip force             │
│                    v                                        │
│              [Control]                                      │
│                    │ Motor torques                          │
│                    v                                        │
│              [Actuators] ──> Physical motion                │
│                                                              │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Figure 2.2: Data flow from sensors through perception, planning, and control to actuators.

Step 1: Perception

  • Cameras capture RGB images; depth sensors provide 3D point clouds
  • Perception algorithms detect the cup, estimate its 3D location, and verify it's graspable
  • The IMU reports the robot's tilt and angular velocity for balance monitoring

Step 2: Planning

  • The task planner receives "cup at position (x, y, z)" from perception
  • The motion planner computes a collision-free arm trajectory from current pose to a pre-grasp position near the cup
  • The grasp planner selects a suitable grasp pose (e.g., fingers around the cup's handle)

Step 3: Control

  • The trajectory from planning is passed to the control layer as a sequence of desired joint positions
  • The joint controller computes motor torques to follow this trajectory smoothly
  • As the arm moves, the balance controller adjusts leg joints to compensate for the arm's changing weight distribution

Step 4: Feedback

  • Sensors continuously provide new data: joint encoders report actual joint angles, force sensors measure contact forces
  • The control layer adjusts motor commands in real-time based on this feedback
  • If the cup is heavier than expected, force feedback prompts the controller to increase grip force

This cycle repeats continuously. Perception updates the world model, planning refines the action sequence, and control executes the current plan—all while adapting to new information.

Perception Subsystem​

Let's dive deeper into the perception subsystem, which transforms raw sensor streams into actionable knowledge.

Types of Sensors​

Humanoid robots use a variety of sensors, categorized by what they measure:

1. Exteroceptive Sensors (measure the external environment):

  • Cameras: RGB images for object recognition, scene understanding
  • Depth Sensors (e.g., stereo cameras, LiDAR, structured light): 3D geometry of the environment
  • Microphones: Audio for speech recognition or sound localization
  • Tactile Sensors: Pressure-sensitive pads on fingers for detecting contact

2. Proprioceptive Sensors (measure the robot's internal state):

  • Joint Encoders: Measure joint angles and velocities
  • Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs): Measure orientation, angular velocity, and linear acceleration
  • Force/Torque Sensors: Measure forces at joints or end-effectors (e.g., wrist force sensors)

Example: A humanoid grasping a cup uses:

  • Cameras to locate the cup visually
  • Depth sensors to estimate its 3D position
  • Joint encoders to know where its arm currently is
  • Tactile sensors on fingertips to detect when contact is made
  • Force sensors to measure grip force and avoid crushing the cup

Perception Algorithms​

Raw sensor data is noisy and high-dimensional. Perception algorithms extract meaning:

  • Object Detection: Identifying objects in images (e.g., "that's a cup")
  • Semantic Segmentation: Labeling every pixel in an image (sky, floor, table, person)
  • Pose Estimation: Determining the 3D position and orientation of objects or the robot itself
  • SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping): Building a map of the environment while tracking the robot's location
  • State Estimation: Fusing sensor data to estimate the robot's state (position, velocity, orientation)

These algorithms run continuously, updating the robot's internal model of the world as it moves and senses.

Planning Subsystem​

The planning subsystem decides what actions to take. It operates at multiple levels:

Task Planning (High-Level)​

Task planning sequences abstract actions to achieve goals. For example, to "prepare coffee," the task planner might generate:

  1. Navigate to kitchen
  2. Locate coffee machine
  3. Grasp coffee pot
  4. Fill pot with water
  5. Place pot in machine
  6. Press start button

Task planning often uses symbolic reasoning, finite state machines, or behavior trees.

Motion Planning (Mid-Level)​

Motion planning computes collision-free trajectories for the robot's body or arms. Given a start configuration and goal configuration, the motion planner searches for a path that:

  • Avoids obstacles (detected by perception)
  • Respects joint limits and kinematic constraints
  • Optimizes for smoothness, speed, or energy

Common algorithms: RRT (Rapidly-exploring Random Trees), PRM (Probabilistic Roadmaps), optimization-based planners.

Behavior Arbitration (Coordination)​

When multiple objectives compete (e.g., "reach goal quickly" vs. "maintain safe distance from humans"), the planning system must arbitrate. This might involve:

  • Priority hierarchies: Safety constraints override performance goals
  • Cost functions: Assign costs to different behaviors and choose the least-cost option
  • Finite state machines: Explicit rules for switching between behaviors (e.g., "if human nearby, switch to cautious mode")

Control Subsystem​

The control subsystem executes planned motions through precise motor control.

Joint-Level Control​

Each joint (hip, knee, elbow, etc.) has a motor controlled by a feedback loop. A simple approach is PID control:

  • Proportional (P): Apply torque proportional to the error (difference between desired and actual angle)
  • Integral (I): Correct for steady-state errors by integrating past errors
  • Derivative (D): Dampen oscillations by reacting to the rate of change of error

PID is widely used but limited. Advanced controllers use model-based methods (e.g., computed torque control, model predictive control) that account for dynamics, friction, and coupling between joints.

Whole-Body Control​

Humanoid robots have many joints (often 30+), and controlling them individually isn't enough. Whole-body control coordinates all joints to achieve complex behaviors like:

  • Walking: Moving legs in a coordinated gait while maintaining balance
  • Reaching: Moving an arm while adjusting the torso and legs to keep the center of mass stable
  • Manipulation: Grasping an object while counteracting the forces exerted

Whole-body controllers use optimization to solve for joint torques that satisfy multiple objectives: reach the target, maintain balance, minimize energy, stay within joint limits.

Balance and Stability​

For humanoid robots, balance is critical. Falling is catastrophic. Balance controllers:

  • Monitor the Zero Moment Point (ZMP): A metric indicating whether the robot is stable
  • Adjust foot placement, ankle torques, or upper body posture to keep the ZMP within a safe region
  • React to disturbances (e.g., a push) by stepping or shifting weight

System Integration: The Role of Middleware​

With so many subsystems—perception running object detection, planning computing paths, control sending motor commands—how do they communicate?

Enter middleware: software frameworks that connect subsystems, manage data flow, and abstract hardware details. The most widely used middleware for robotics is ROS 2 (Robot Operating System 2), which we'll explore in detail in Module 1.

What Middleware Provides​

1. Communication: Subsystems run as separate processes (nodes) and exchange messages over topics. For example:

  • Perception publishes detected objects to a /objects topic
  • Planning subscribes to /objects and publishes trajectories to a /trajectory topic
  • Control subscribes to /trajectory and sends motor commands to actuators

2. Hardware Abstraction: Middleware provides a uniform interface to sensors and actuators. Whether using a specific camera model or joint controller, the software interface remains consistent.

3. Modularity: You can swap components (e.g., replace one camera with another, or upgrade the motion planner) without rewriting the entire system.

4. Real-Time Support: ROS 2 (unlike ROS 1) supports real-time communication, critical for control loops that must run at 100+ Hz with guaranteed latency.

Example: A Complete Humanoid System​

Imagine a humanoid robot navigating a room. Here's a simplified view of its subsystems communicating via ROS 2:

  • Camera Node: Publishes images to /camera/image
  • Perception Node: Subscribes to /camera/image, detects obstacles, publishes to /obstacles
  • Navigation Node: Subscribes to /obstacles, plans a path, publishes to /cmd_vel (velocity commands)
  • Control Node: Subscribes to /cmd_vel, computes joint commands, publishes to /joint_commands
  • Motor Node: Subscribes to /joint_commands, sends signals to physical motors

Each node runs independently, and middleware ensures messages flow smoothly between them. This modularity makes the system easier to develop, test, and debug.

Summary​

In this chapter, we examined the three-layer architecture that structures humanoid robots:

  • Perception Layer: Sensors and algorithms that sense the environment and estimate state
  • Planning Layer: Decision-making systems that determine what actions to take
  • Control Layer: Feedback controllers that execute actions through motor commands

We traced the data flow from sensors (cameras, IMUs, joint encoders) through perception (object detection, state estimation) to planning (task planning, motion planning) and finally to control (joint control, whole-body control) and actuators (motors, grippers).

We also introduced the concept of middleware—software like ROS 2 that integrates subsystems, enabling modular, scalable robotic systems.

Key takeaways:

  • Humanoid robots are systems of subsystems, each responsible for a layer of intelligence
  • Data flows continuously from sensors to actuators, with feedback loops at every level
  • Middleware (especially ROS 2) is essential for coordinating complex, multi-component systems
  • Real-time constraints demand efficient communication and control at high frequencies

With this architectural understanding, we're ready to dive deeper into the middleware that makes it all work. In Module 1, we'll explore ROS 2 in detail, starting with its core concepts of nodes, topics, and messages.
